A handheld disease-and-pest scouting device for durian orchards

Founder & lead engineer, 2025 – present · pre-incorporation venture, Malaysia

A grower walks the orchard, photographs a suspect leaf or trunk, and within a few hundred milliseconds the device suggests which of 12 disease and pest categories it might be. It runs entirely on a battery-powered Raspberry Pi 5 — no network, nothing uploaded — and is a decision aid, not a diagnosis: the final call stays with the grower.

I built the whole thing: field data collection across 11 orchard sites in Peninsular Malaysia and Sabah, the lesion-level annotation standard (set with a crop-protection practitioner), detector training, ONNX / LiteRT export, quantisation, and on-device profiling with hardware counters. It is a field-tested prototype; trials with growers are the next step.

One model or two? Deciding by what the operator actually waits for

Raspberry Pi 5 · ONNX Runtime · YOLO11s / YOLO11n · Arm PMU counters · battery-rail power

The fielded system ran two specialist detectors (diseases, pests) fused at run time. I trained a unified 12-class detector and compared them as deployable pipelines, not as models: same 247-image evaluation set, same decoding, fusion policy swept, three seeds, paired bootstrap.

  • Accuracy did not separate them: 0.007 macro AP@0.5, a third of the seed-to-seed spread; the fusion rule alone moved the specialist score across 0.351–0.456.
  • The pair cost 2.05× the unified model's latency and introduced a cross-model arbitration error that no aggregate metric shows.
  • A small second detector overshot its arithmetic-proportional cost by 41%, because per-pass memory traffic on a 2 MB-cache SoC doesn't scale with FLOPs.
  • Under the real on-demand duty cycle, per-patrol energy differed by 2%; operator wait differed by 2×. The device moved to one model: 718 → 351 ms.

Which categories survive INT8? A per-class deployability screen

LiteRT + XNNPACK · FP32 / w8a32 / w8a16 / INT8 · 4 backbones · 72 Wh battery rail · PMU counters

INT8 is the default before deployment: 2.9–4.2× faster and 71–79% less energy per image on this device. Aggregate mAP fell 31–53%, which a team might accept — but scored per class, INT8 dropped the two smallest pest categories below a usable bound on every backbone. Those were the two categories the device exists to catch.

  • Weight precision wasn't the problem: weight-only (dynamic) quantisation kept 96–99% of FP32 accuracy at FP32 latency and 24–40% lower energy, and removed no category on 3 of 4 backbones.
  • Median target area, measured from the labels alone, predicts which classes fall (Spearman −0.85; −0.94 on VisDrone) — so the at-risk classes are known before training.
  • Two export paths from identical weights differed 750× in output scale; one zeroed every class score while the boxes stayed correct. The same INT8 file scored 0.339 vs 0.064 under two runtime versions.

Thermal cut-off, not throttling, is what stops the device paper accepted

Raspberry Pi 5 + Active Cooler · YOLOv8s · 5 duty-cycle configurations · 3 h runs, replicated 4 weeks apart · SoC power rails

Everyone worries about frequency throttling. On this platform it cost 2.6% of median latency. What interrupted monitoring was the 82 °C software cut-off: 99 events in 3 hours of continuous inference, each a 5 s gap during which trees were walked past unexamined. A 30 s sleep interval removed them under test conditions; 45 s kept a margin.

  • Duty cycling lowered mean SoC power by 28% but raised energy per inference by 39%, because the idle floor is paid through every sleep — a trade a battery budget has to know about.
  • A dataset-integrity audit (MD5 + perceptual hashing) found an augment-before-split leak that had inflated one class's AP by 35% and mAP by 9.5%; the split was rebuilt at source-photo level.
  • Eleven runs across three detector families left the same two classes at zero — capacity wasn't the cause; one was never labelled, one was a property of the validation sample.

Scheduling as a thermal actuator: 12.7 °C from a software change

Two co-located detectors (YOLO11s leaf, YOLO11n pest) · parallel / staggered / sequential · 3 h per policy · 63,952 telemetry samples

Running two detectors at the same time on a passively cooled node pushed the die to 81.0 °C, one degree from the cut-off. Running them one after the other peaked at 68.3 °C — same models, same frame rate, no hardware change — and cut pest-model latency by 33%.

  • Concurrency didn't add a fixed penalty; it contaminated the body of the latency distribution: median unchanged, interquartile range 184 → 52 ms after serialising.
  • The slow inferences recurred on the beat period of the two invocation schedules (~13 s), which a fixed start-offset delays but cannot prevent.
  • An INT8 accelerator (Hailo-8L) ran 15× faster and returned ~188 boxes per empty frame; the GPU-dependent conversion path was judged unfit for an evolving label space and the device was returned.

A premium tier on Jetson, and why I dropped it

Jetson Orin Nano Super vs Raspberry Pi 5 · same two ONNX detectors · ONNX Runtime CUDA vs CPU · Docker · 3 h battery runs with pack telemetry

The plan was two products: Jetson for growers who want the fastest device, Pi 5 for everyone else. I ported the whole pipeline to Jetson and ran both boards through the same scheduling trials and on battery before deciding.

  • Jetson was 5.4× faster on pest detection (186 → 35 ms) at 2.9× the hardware cost and about 1.4× the power at the pack (11.7 W vs 8.2 W), so on a common 60 Wh pack it would last 5.1 h instead of 7.3 h. Energy per detection cycle was about the same (4.44 vs 4.20 mWh): the speed bought no energy saving per unit of work.
  • A hand-built CUDA container hit three faults in a row: a CPU-only ONNX Runtime that the dependency installer substituted silently, a PyTorch wheel whose version string pip rejected, and an ARM SVE detection fault. The vendor-maintained image was what worked, and GPU containers on this board carry far more setup risk than CPU ones.
  • Where the battery goes on the Pi: pausing inference while capture kept running showed 6.3 W between inferences against 8.4 W inferring, so a faster model can save at most about half the energy; 1.6 W of the idle draw is the camera loop.
  • Decision: the board needed repeated OS reflashes, which a grower cannot do in the field, and Pi 5 already met the response-time target. The Jetson tier was dropped.

A containerised greenhouse monitoring node — and why container-overhead numbers don't travel

Urban Farm Tech internship, Jan–May 2026 · MobileNetV2 → ONNX · Docker vs native · 4 interleaved 3 h runs · UPS-rail power

Built and deployed an offline basil disease-monitoring node in a commercial greenhouse: ~1,700 on-site images, Raspberry Pi 5, Telegram alerts confirmed over 3-of-5 frames, under 2.5 s end to end. Then measured what Docker actually cost.

  • The container drew 0.47 W less and ran 3.7 °C cooler — but completed 6.6% fewer inferences, so energy per inference was not detectably better. Report energy per unit of work, not watts.
  • Its 46% higher latency traced to an ONNX Runtime version mismatch baked into the image (1.19 vs 1.29 on the host), not to containerisation itself. Wrote a per-run environment-manifest tool so such comparisons stay attributable.
  • Over 311,683 consecutive frames of a quiet scene the node produced zero spurious alerts; the four novel objects that did appear were all routed to one class — the case for an explicit reject option.

Capture sessions, not images: what a field accuracy figure actually measures

560 field images → 73 capture sessions · 9 architectures × 4 seeds · leave-one-farm-out on 8 orchards · PlantVillage leaf IDs

A camera carried into an orchard produces many near-independent-looking photos of one tree. Split at image level, 96.7% of test images shared a session with training; macro-F1 was inflated by 12.2 points on average and the ranking of nine architectures changed. PlantVillage has the same structure: 40,490 images are 7,524 leaves, and an image-level split leaks 99.6% of its test set.

  • Hash- and DINOv2-based audits recovered almost none of the session structure, so the grouping has to be recorded at capture — it can't be rebuilt after release.
  • Held out by farm, which farm is scored explains 70–88% of the variance in the reported figure; the architecture explains 0–3%. Using the withheld farm to pick the checkpoint moved figures by up to 14.7 points.
  • Practical consequence for the device: collect from more orchards before touching the model, and validate on orchards it has never seen.
